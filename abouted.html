About the creator

Max Jann

I write software, songs, and stories. Sometimes I go off the deep end, and people forget how good I am at swimming.

Creator

I was born in Princeton, New Jersey, on September 30, 1991. My first car was a silver 2005 Mustang. I studied philosophy at UC Santa Cruz, learned full-stack development at Coding Dojo, and these days I work as an engineering technician at Applied Materials.

One thing my résumé leaves out is that I represented myself in a case against AMD and ZT Systems, using AI as my paralegal. Ironically, I think I was a better lawyer than I was a client.

For a different introduction, play through one of my chess games or hear what my singing voice sounds like.

Website

I hand-coded maxjann.com before ChatGPT existed. I wanted a place where a song, a chess game, and a chapter of my book could all live under the same roof. Building that place became a project of its own.

I think of that original code as the bones. Working with AI, I’m giving it a nervous system. When I win a rapid game, my chess page can pick it up. When I return to a project, I can water it in Seed to Sky and see which ideas have gone a little dry. The site is starting to keep up with the person who built it.

Mythology

Interbeautiful is an autofictional sci-fi love story. It begins with a question a man asks his girlfriend because he means to spend his life with her. What does she want to do? She wants to do what he wants to do. By the time they finish recording his answer, she knows that saying yes could cost them that life together.

I call him the Founder. He has a theory that intelligence reveals itself in the beauty a person can bring into their life. The whole theory begins with her. He loves her face. He loves that when he asks what she wants, she takes his own wish into account. To him, being loved by a woman of such physical and spiritual beauty is the luckiest thing that has ever happened to him. Then a less comfortable thought occurs to him. Suppose some gift of his helped bring them together. How much of that gift is he entitled to spend on his own happiness?

He makes two voice memos. He is trying to say, as precisely as he knows how, something that could alter the course of human history. His entire audience, for now, is one woman. She knows the cop he wants to hear the recordings. He has a hunch the cop could get them to someone inside an intelligence agency. He could be wrong about that connection. The one part he knows for certain is that he can talk into a phone all night and the message will travel exactly as far as she chooses to carry it.

What he asks for on tape is a fair test. Have people describe, in their own words, the person they would trust to lead this work. Collect the answers before showing them the candidates. Compare the descriptions blind. Try it several ways. He expects his name to come up, but half of him is hoping for the pleasure of being introduced to a better man. If someone else wins, he wants that meeting. If he wins, the recordings commit him in advance to what he calls his donation.

He calls his habit of answering objections before anyone can raise them profilaxis. He recognizes his priveldged position and desires to be stripped of his inessential support systems, and to surrender his privacy to be studied for unparalled research of minor infinities of personal data. He believes people will be more willing to join an organization that doesn't benefit the founder. (see Christianity).

After all, if the founder is profitting from the growth of the organization directly, it might be a pyramid scheme. So he offers to surrender his own memory of the plan and let the recordings speak for him. His design has a peculiar advantage and a genuine flaw. It takes a minute to hear a claim about what a man will do with his life. It may take years to watch him do it. Until then, a skeptic has good reason to withhold judgment. The Founder has also bought himself years in which nobody can settle the argument. He also uses various riddles for the short term, taking advantage of elements of his life that have always struck him as non-coincidental.

He offers them the one thing he can keep giving after he’s forgotten the plan—his next move. He fears he might have the fragility of lightning in a bottle, so he asks them to build a computer model of his brain and put it to a test. Let the model write down its prediction before he makes his choice. As long as he’s alive, they can compare the two. Someday, he hopes, the model will face a decision he never lived to see. That’s the decision he’s doing this for. (see Artificial Intelligence)

She understands what the donation could mean for her. If the work calls for them to be separated, she may have to live apart from a man who no longer remembers making the choice with her. She agrees to carry the recordings. He hopes the gift that brought two people together might help a whole group act together, even after its founder is gone. She is the first person who gets to weigh that hope against an actual life she wants. The life is hers.

That is where the battery in this mythology comes from. I picture the two of them as its positive and negative terminals, held apart until there is somewhere for the current to go. The chemical reaction is true love. The electrical energy is this website. On my side of the screen that has meant years of writing songs, learning to code, and coming back to a scene because I still care what she heard in his voice. On your side it has carried two people in a silver Mustang all the way to wherever you are reading this.

Some scenes come back to me with the texture of memory. I call them pseudomemories. I can go to work, come home, and write the scene as I remember it. The Founder once asked whom he could surrender to. My own answer now is Christ. Someday I want to make the film, and before you hear either voice on the recording, I want these words on the screen.

“Based on a false memory.”

If the memory is false, can the story still be true?
